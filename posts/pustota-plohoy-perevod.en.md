# Emptiness Is a Bad Translation

I sat down to work out what consciousness is. I was looking for the part of it that is me.

Didn't find it.

Buddhist theory gives an uncomfortable answer: there is no indivisible seed inside. A person is a set of elements that influence each other and produce the sensation of a personality. Single, whole, the same one as yesterday. The sensation is convincing. There is nothing underneath it.

Most people are sure of the opposite. Something is in there — a soul, an essence, a core, "I'm just that kind of person." Take it away and a hole is left. Buddhism says the hole was there from the start, and that this is not a tragedy but the design.

## There Are No Things, Only Processes

The thesis is easy in words and hard in practice: there are no objects, there are processes influencing each other. Since the Big Bang that mutual influence has never stopped. What caused it is unknown. But from that point on, everything that happens happens in gear with everything else.

The Buddhist term for this is śūnyatā. It's usually translated as "emptiness." Emptiness here isn't about nothing existing. It's about no thing containing its own independent, unchanging essence. Take a chariot apart into wheels, axle and body, and you won't find any separate "chariotness" anywhere. The chariot exists, but it is a label for a relation between parts, not an object sitting on top of them.

## The Bridge That Almost Holds

When I got this far, I remembered that we have our own word for a similar situation — emergence. Several objects combine into a system, and the system gets properties none of the parts had. The wetness of water isn't inside a single molecule. A personality isn't inside a single neuron.

The bridge works. It shows anyone with scientific habits that "the whole is not the sum of its parts" is not a mystical idea. It just gets you to the middle of the river.

Emergence leaves the foundation in place. The scientific picture has a base level — particles, fields, laws — and that level is not itself emergent. New things are built on top of something stable. Śūnyatā says nothing stable exists on any floor. Particles are dependent, so are space and time, so is causality itself. Nagarjuna takes arising apart and shows that neither "from itself," nor "from another," nor "from both," nor "without a cause" survives analysis. Dig as long as you like — there is no bottom.

Space deserves a separate note, because intuition lies hardest here. We picture it as an empty box that exists on its own, and then someone placed stars, planets and us inside the box. Twentieth-century physics broke that picture. For Einstein space and time aren't the stage, they're a participant: they aren't given in advance, they're tied to matter and energy. Mass curves the geometry, the geometry tells the mass how to move. There is no empty box that would exist before things and apart from them. There are relations between things, and distance is a property of those relations, not of the box.

The second difference matters more. Emergence describes the design; śūnyatā delivers a diagnosis: your suffering grows out of not understanding that design.

## Isn't It the Same Plug?

The most honest objection to this whole text goes like this. Emergence is a plug for what we don't know. Why does consciousness arise? Because of emergence. The word stands in the place of an explanation and explains nothing.

Emptiness can become exactly the same plug. And often does. Why are things the way they are? Because they're empty. The same empty work.

The difference is structural. Emergence is affirmative: it posits a new property at a new level. Śūnyatā is negative: it posits nothing, it removes a mistaken assumption. Nagarjuna insists he has no thesis of his own, only refutations of other people's. And he applies the negation to itself: emptiness is also empty. Not an essence, not a substance, not "the stuff everything is made of."

The trap isn't in the word but in how you use it: as an explanation it's a plug, as a negation it's a tool.

A separate word on physics, so nobody drives off into esoterics. Quantum mechanics has an effect that comes up first in every conversation about interconnectedness — entanglement. The point of it is this: if two particles once interacted, you can no longer honestly describe them separately, the description becomes shared, one for both. The early universe was dense and hot, everything in it was in contact with everything, so formally it can be treated as one shared quantum system whose parts aren't fully independent of each other.

That sounds like ready-made confirmation of everything above. It doesn't work as confirmation, for two reasons. First: entanglement is not a wire. You can't send a signal through it, and you can't pull one end and change something at the other. The correlation can't even be noticed until you compare the results of two measurements, and comparing them happens the ordinary way, at the speed of light or slower. Second: as soon as a particle touches its environment — light, air, heat — its entanglement is smeared across billions of particles of that environment and stops being accessible. This is called decoherence, and it's exactly why no quantum interconnectedness is observed in the world of chairs, people and cups.

Physics gives a nice rhyme here, but not a proof. These are different conversations: one about how measurement works, the other about whether things have an essence of their own.

## A Bad Word

The main damage is the translation itself. "Emptiness" drags along its everyday meaning: nothing exists, it's all pointless, hands drop. Śūnyatā means dependent arising. Sometimes it's translated as "relativity" — closer, but still off. The word is native to its own language and doesn't translate correctly at all.

## What to Do With It

The practical payoff showed up for me not from reading but after regular vipassana — a practice whose whole point is to see from experience that everything appears, lasts and after a while disappears. I got calmer and more confident.

The mechanics go like this. Stress comes not from the event but from the mind sculpting a static thing out of a current state. "I'm an anxious person." "This will always be like this." What frightens is not the pain but the imagined immobility, and resistance to it settles on top of the original pain as a second layer. When you see a state as a process, a stream of changing moments, the resistance loses its footing: the premise "this will never end" falls apart. It works not as a thought but as observation — when right now you notice the sensation is already different from half a minute ago.

It also settles a question that held me for a long time. If everything has been in gear since the first impulse, where is free will. The only freedom we have is choosing how to react to circumstances. The overwhelming majority of circumstances don't depend on us.

That's where the practical meaning ends: don't look for a seed inside that isn't there, and watch how a sensation changes while you observe it.
