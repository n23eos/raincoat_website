# Freedoms We Haven't Learned to Think About Yet

I was thinking about AGI. About what an entity thousands or millions of times smarter than us could bring into our lives. And, along the way, about the new kinds of freedom we'd get by pushing our technology up by orders of magnitude.

Then the question flipped: what *can't* we do? The list turned out to be short and very uncomfortable.

I can't walk into the mountains or the desert and stay there for months without worrying about food first. Not gear, not permits, not visas — food. A sack of calories I have to carry on my back. That's the boundary of my freedom: it runs along the volume of my backpack.

And I can't stop thinking about money. Whether I eat today, whether I sleep in my own house or on the street, depends on it. The chain here is literal: no money, no food, no me.

Neither of those limits is written into any law. You can't vote them away.

## Freedom Isn't "I'm Allowed To"

When people say "freedom," they almost always mean politics. Permitted or forbidden. Someone above either grants it or doesn't.

But look at the whole space of states available to a human being and the definition comes out differently. Freedom is the set of world-states I'm able to consciously choose and realize. Not how much I'm permitted, but how much is physically reachable.

Then everything falls into place. The car expanded the space of reachable places. The internet — the space of reachable information and people. Money — the space of available actions. Medicine — the space of available body states. Spaceflight — getting past the planet.

Every one of those expands the set. Convenience is a side effect.

And then the list of constraints looks different. Everything needed to keep a human body in working order: temperature, environment, food, water, oxygen, sleep, safety, no radiation, no toxins. Plus disease, aging, death. The body requires a fairly narrow range of conditions and shuts down outside it. Everything else is superstructure.

## Near Freedoms: Labor and Scarcity

For most of history the link was direct. Don't work, no food, death. No philosophy involved, just the physics of metabolism.

Automation is gradually tearing that link apart. If machines produce food, energy, housing, transport and most services with almost no human labor, freedom from economic necessity appears. This isn't about a right not to work. It's that survival stops requiring you to sell a large share of your time.

Next to it sits freedom from scarcity, which is even more interesting, because scarcity is the source of most forced choices. No energy, no desalinated water. No cheap compute, no machine intelligence. No materials, expensive construction. No food, and people fight over land.

Less scarcity, fewer forced compromises, more available options. Which is why cheap energy and robotics don't file under economics for me. They file under technologies of freedom.

## Cognitive Freedom: Already Running

The brain is a set of constraints too. We forget. We learn slowly. We can't hold a hundred variables at once. We're prone to bias. We struggle to pass complex thoughts to each other.

And right now people with attention disorders have raised their capabilities through AI. Not because they got smarter. Because the dullest part of the work — exactly the part that has been killing the launch of every project for years — can be handed to agents. The barrier physically dropped.

That's the first step of a long staircase: memory → external memory → AI assistant → a permanent cognitive exocortex → neural interface. We're standing on the first step and it already works, noticeably.

## Body and Environment: The One That Grabs Me Most

Out of the whole list, this one is mine.

Freedom from the constraints of the physical body. From existing in organic form with all its logistics: food, water, sleep, temperature, risk.

Why do I have two arms? Why does vision catch a tiny slice of the electromagnetic spectrum? Why can't I see infrared, hear ultrasound, feel Earth's magnetic field, or run ten robots at once? Not because physics forbids it. Evolution just handed Homo sapiens one particular configuration and we took it for ourselves.

Neural interfaces, prosthetics and synthetic biology lead from "this body was given to me" to "I choose what my body can do." That's morphological freedom.

Right beside it is freedom from environment. The chain looks like this: human → spacesuit → base → artificial habitat → modified organism → an organism able to live in conditions it wasn't built for. Today, without water in the desert, you die in a couple of days. A future human could have a different metabolism, internal reserves, artificial organs, or not be fully biological at all.

That's when I'll go into the mountains for six months. Without a bag of food.

## Far Freedoms: Planet, Time, Form

Right now all of humanity lives in the thinnest film on the surface of one planet. Earth → Moon → Mars → asteroids → orbital settlements → other star systems. But the real jump won't come when a state can send a human to Mars. It comes when an ordinary person can pick Mars roughly the way they pick another country today. Everything sits in the gap between "a nation was sent" and "I went."

Then time. You can choose a profession, a country, a partner, a project. But there's a hard timer: a few decades of active life, and that's it. If aging can be slowed or reversed, temporal freedom appears, and it changes absolutely everything. "It'll take me thirty years to learn this" stops being a verdict and becomes a schedule.

The extreme version of the same idea is freedom from death. Organ replacement, biological immortality, cryonics, digital continuation of a person. Completely different directions, mostly speculative, but they run into one boundary. Into nature's right to end your existence regardless of what you want.

And further still, freedom from a single form. Today one person is one brain, one body, one place. Hypothetically, one identity across many carriers. At that point the question "where is this person right now?" starts losing its usual meaning. Plus virtual environments, where part of the physical world's constraints become programmable. Don't like gravity, change it. Need a room the size of a planet, build it.

## The Staircase

Lay it all out in order and it comes out like this.

Political freedom — "I'm allowed." Economic — "I have resources." Technological — "I have tools." Biological — "my body no longer gets in the way." Cognitive — "my brain is no longer the ceiling." Spatial — "I'm not tied to one planet." Temporal — "the length of my life isn't fixed." Morphological — "my form isn't fixed." And existential — "the very way I exist becomes something I choose."

Each step makes the previous one look small. We've been arguing on the first step for a few thousand years and treating it as the main subject.

## Two Objections Worth Hearing

First: once a person no longer has to satisfy natural needs, meaning will disappear. Death gives life its dramatic dimension; without it everything loses value.

Well then. If there's ever a transition to a form of existence free of physiology and death, let people have the choice. Some will stay. Maybe someone wants their body to shrivel, their skin to wrinkle, every step to come harder. Maybe someone wants to age, and that's their right — freedom to age is freedom too. But I think a fairly large share of people will agree to be immortal, permanently healthy, and independent of whether there's food or water nearby.

The argument about meaning matters exactly up to the point where a choice exists. After that it's each person's own business, not an argument against the possibility itself.

The second objection is more serious: all of this will go to the rich, and the gap will become a chasm.

But the whole line of reasoning assumes AGI, a superintelligence, capable of generating an enormous volume of goods that ordinary people can't currently imagine. In a world where producing goods costs almost nothing, restricting access to them makes no sense. A privileged group will remain, of course. But its privilege will be in visibility, influence on society, participation in key decisions — not in comfort. Comfort will be available to everyone.

That's a testable claim, by the way. If basic goods get more expensive rather than cheaper over the next twenty years, I was wrong.

## What Follows

What comes out of this is a criterion for judging technology without reading tea leaves: progress is good to the degree that it expands the space of voluntarily available human possibilities. Not by convenience, not by profit. Did a person gain more states they can choose?

By that criterion it's immediately obvious what's worth building and what's a pretty shell.

If I ever start a company or a nonprofit, that will be its mission. Not "human rights" in the familiar packaging, but the systematic removal of constraints on human agency: economic, physical, biological, cognitive, and ultimately planetary.

And the question I started with hasn't gone anywhere. I thought I was thinking about what AGI would be capable of. It turns out I'd been thinking the whole time about what humans would stop being incapable of.

Which is why the arrival of superintelligence mostly makes me glad. Not because it will do everything for us. Because it's the only instrument I know of that can lift constraints on that scale.

I want to live to see it. I want to walk a distant empty planet for years, build impossible structures with robots, and stop depending on one single organic body, fragile and very demanding about its surroundings.
